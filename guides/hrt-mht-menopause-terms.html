HRT, MHT or HT? Menopause terms explained for the UK, Australia and Canada

Updated October 2026

General information, not medical advice. This guide is about keeping a record and preparing for an appointment. It cannot tell you whether you are in perimenopause, and it does not recommend any treatment. Speak to your GP, nurse or doctor about your own symptoms, and get help promptly for anything that worries you.

Read about menopause online and you'll meet the same thing under different names depending on which country the page comes from. That's confusing when you're trying to work out what a leaflet, a forum post or your own doctor means. Here is a plain glossary of the terms you're most likely to see, and who uses which.

HRT, MHT and HT: same family, different names

  • HRT — hormone replacement therapy. The usual term in the UK. The NHS and the NICE guideline both use it (NHS: HRT, NICE NG23).
  • MHT — menopausal hormone therapy. The usual term in Australia. Jean Hailes describes MHT as also being called HRT (Jean Hailes), and the Australasian Menopause Society uses MHT throughout. In Canada, the Canadian Menopause Society also publishes its materials under "MHT".
  • HT — hormone therapy. Used by Menopause and U, the patient site of the Society of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists of Canada.

When you see any of these, the writer is talking about the same broad group of prescribed hormone treatments for menopause symptoms. Which specific treatment, if any, is right for you is a conversation for your doctor — the name on the page doesn't change that.

Stages

  • Perimenopause — the time when periods become irregular before they stop (NHS). Jean Hailes describes it as running from when periods start changing or symptoms begin until a year after the final period, and says that in Australia it usually starts around age 47 and lasts 4 to 6 years on average, sometimes shorter or longer.
  • Menopause — confirmed after 12 months without a period, if you're not using hormonal contraception. It usually happens between about 45 and 55 (NHS). Jean Hailes gives the average age of natural menopause in Australia as 51.
  • Postmenopause — the stage after menopause. The NHS notes symptoms usually last 7 to 9 years, sometimes longer, and can change over that time (NHS: symptoms).
  • Early menopause — menopause between 40 and 44, in NICE's definition (NICE NG23).
  • Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) — the term NICE uses for menopause before 40, which has its own section of the guideline.

Symptoms

  • Hot flush / hot flash — the same thing. "Flush" is usual in the UK and Australia, "flash" in the US and Canada. The NHS describes the face, neck and chest suddenly feeling very hot, sometimes with sweating, palpitations or dizziness.
  • Night sweats — hot flushes that happen at night, often waking you.
  • Vasomotor symptoms — the clinical umbrella term for hot flushes and night sweats, used in the NICE guideline. If you see "VMS", this is it.
  • Brain fog — problems with memory and concentration, listed by the NHS among menopause symptoms.

Tests and hormones

  • FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) — a hormone that can be measured with a blood test. NICE explains that in people aged 45 and over FSH tests don't help identify perimenopause, because levels go up and down during this time (NICE QS143). For some younger people a test may be considered.
  • Oestrogen / estrogen — the same hormone. "Oestrogen" is the UK and Australian spelling, "estrogen" the North American one.

Who to see

  • GP — in the UK and Australia, your first port of call. In Canada, a family doctor or nurse practitioner.
  • Menopause specialist — the British Menopause Society, the Australasian Menopause Society and the Menopause Foundation of Canada each provide ways to find clinicians with a menopause interest (BMS, AMS, MFC).

Why the words matter for you

If you search using your own country's term, you'll find guidance written for your health system: "HRT" will bring up NHS pages, "MHT" Australian ones. And when you keep a record of your symptoms or treatments, using the same words your doctor uses makes the conversation quicker. Our GP appointment guide covers what to bring.
