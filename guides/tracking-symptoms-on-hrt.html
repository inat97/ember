Started HRT or MHT? What to track before your review

Updated October 2026 · Guidance current as of October 2026

General information, not medical advice. This guide is about keeping a record and preparing for an appointment. It cannot tell you whether you are in perimenopause, and it does not recommend any treatment. Speak to your GP, nurse or doctor about your own symptoms, and get help promptly for anything that worries you.

If you and your doctor have decided to start hormone therapy — called HRT in the UK, and often MHT (menopausal hormone therapy) in Australia and Canada — you will usually be asked how you are getting on at a review a few months later. "I think it's a bit better?" is hard to work with. A simple record, kept from before you start through to that review, lets you answer with what actually happened.

This guide is only about recording. Whether to start, continue, change or stop a treatment is a decision for you and your prescriber.

What the guidance says about reviews

  • UK, NICE: the NG23 guideline recommends reviewing each treatment for menopause symptoms at 3 months, to look at how well it is working and how well it is tolerated, and then once a year unless there is a reason to review sooner (NICE NG23, recommendation 1.9.2, as of October 2026).
  • UK, NHS: the NHS says you will usually have a review of HRT every year (NHS: when to take HRT).
  • Australia: the Australasian Menopause Society's symptom score card has three columns — before starting MHT, 3 months after and 6 months after — which is a ready-made layout for a before-and-after record (AMS symptom score card, PDF).

Your own prescriber may set a different schedule. Go by what they tell you.

Before you start: a baseline

A comparison needs a "before". If you have been keeping a symptom diary, you already have one. If not, start recording the day you get your prescription — don't delay treatment just to collect data. Note the same things you'll track afterwards: hot flushes, night sweats, sleep, mood and anything else that bothers you, each on the same 0–3 scale (our symptom diary guide has a template).

What to write down once you start

  • The start date, and the product, form (patch, gel, spray, tablet) and dose exactly as prescribed — copy it from the label.
  • Any change to the product or dose, with the date. Each change starts a new "after" period.
  • Missed doses or patches that came off.
  • Your usual symptoms, on the same scale as your baseline.
  • Bleeding: dates, how heavy, spotting.
  • Anything new you notice. The NHS lists possible side effects of oestrogen and progestogen such as headaches, breast tenderness, nausea, mood changes, leg cramps and unexpected bleeding or spotting (NHS: HRT side effects).
  • Other changes at the same time: a new supplement, cutting out alcohol, a new exercise routine, a stressful month. These make before-and-after comparisons harder to read, so it helps to have them written down.

When to contact your GP rather than wait

The NHS says side effects usually improve over time and often settle within a few weeks, but advises speaking to a GP if side effects are severe or carry on for longer than 3 months. It says irregular bleeding usually settles within 6 months, and to speak to a GP if it carries on beyond that or becomes heavier (NHS: HRT side effects).

NICE amended its advice on bleeding in April 2026. For people with a womb, it says vaginal bleeding is a common side effect during the first 6 months of systemic HRT and within 3 months of changing the dose or preparation, that it will be asked about at the 3-month review, and that you should seek medical help promptly for unscheduled bleeding beyond those timeframes (NICE NG23, recommendation 1.8.4, as of October 2026). A dated bleeding record makes it easy to see which side of those timeframes you are on. If you're unsure, ask — don't wait for the review.

Reading your own before-and-after honestly

Comparing four weeks before with four weeks after is useful, but keep its limits in mind:

  • Perimenopause symptoms rise and fall on their own, so some change would happen anyway.
  • A summer heatwave, a holiday or a hard month at work can move the numbers.
  • Different lengths of time aren't comparable — compare like with like.

Your diary shows what changed, not why. Bring the numbers and let your prescriber help you interpret them alongside everything else they know about you.

Supplements and lifestyle changes

The same approach works for anything you start — a supplement, a sleep routine, a change to caffeine or alcohol. Record the start date and keep logging. The NHS advises talking to a doctor before taking herbal supplements or complementary medicines (NHS: things you can do), so mention them at your review too.

Questions to bring to the review

  • Here is how my symptoms looked before and since — what do you make of it?
  • Is the bleeding pattern I've recorded within what you'd expect?
  • What should I keep tracking, and when is my next review?
