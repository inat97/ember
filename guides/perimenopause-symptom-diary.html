Perimenopause symptom diary: what to write down each day

Updated October 2026 · For the UK, Australia and Canada

General information, not medical advice. This guide is about keeping a record and preparing for an appointment. It cannot tell you whether you are in perimenopause, and it does not recommend any treatment. Speak to your GP, nurse or doctor about your own symptoms, and get help promptly for anything that worries you.

Perimenopause symptoms come and go, change over time and often overlap with ordinary stress, poor sleep or a busy month. That makes them hard to describe from memory. A simple daily diary solves this: two minutes each evening, and after a few weeks you have something you — and your doctor — can actually read.

Why a diary, and for how long

There is usually no single test for perimenopause. The NHS says a doctor can generally go by your age and symptoms (NHS), and UK NICE guidance describes identifying perimenopause in people aged 45 and over from symptoms and cycle changes rather than lab tests (NICE NG23). So the quality of your description matters.

The Menopause Foundation of Canada recommends tracking your symptoms and periods for at least three months before your appointment (Take Charge of Your Care). Jean Hailes in Australia suggests a detailed journal for at least three months if you can't use your periods as a guide — for example if you have a hormonal IUD or have had a hysterectomy (Jean Hailes).

Which symptoms to include

Hot flushes get the attention, but they are only part of the picture. The NHS lists, among others (NHS: symptoms):

  • changes to periods — more or less often, heavier or lighter
  • hot flushes and night sweats
  • difficulty sleeping
  • low mood, mood changes, anxiety, poor memory and brain fog
  • vaginal dryness and discomfort, and needing to pee more often or urinary infections
  • palpitations, headaches, and muscle and joint pain
  • dry, itchy skin and thinning hair

The Menopause Foundation of Canada says more than 30 symptoms are associated with perimenopause and menopause (MFC: menopause symptoms). You don't need to track all of them. Pick the five to eight that actually affect you, plus a free-text line for anything else.

If you want a structured list, the Australasian Menopause Society publishes a symptom score card (the modified Greene scale) with 21 symptoms, each rated none, mild, moderate or severe. The card itself says it is for documenting symptoms and should not be used to diagnose perimenopause or menopause (AMS symptom score card, PDF). Jean Hailes also has a printable perimenopause and menopause symptom checklist to take to your doctor.

A daily template

Use one line or one small card per day:

  • Date
  • Period: none / spotting / light / medium / heavy
  • Hot flushes: a count, plus the worst one (0–3)
  • Night sweats: 0–3, and whether they woke you
  • Sleep: very poor / poor / okay / good / very good
  • Mood and anxiety: 0–3 each
  • Brain fog: 0–3
  • Aches: 0–3
  • Your own extras: headaches, palpitations, bladder symptoms — whatever applies
  • Notes: anything unusual — a missed dose, a new medicine, illness, a very stressful day, alcohol or a late coffee

The NHS mentions spicy food, caffeine, hot drinks, smoking and alcohol as possible triggers for hot flushes (NHS: things you can do), so a quick note of those can be useful later. Keep it to a word or two; the point is a record, not homework.

Five habits that make a diary useful

  1. Use the same scale every day. 0 none, 1 mild, 2 moderate, 3 severe is the same scale the AMS score card uses, and it's easy to remember.
  2. Log at the same time. The evening works for most people; night sweats can be added the next morning.
  3. Record good days. A day marked "nothing to report" is very different from a day you forgot. Without it, gaps look like either.
  4. Count, don't guess. "About 6 hot flushes" is more useful than "lots".
  5. Don't interpret. Write what happened, not what you think caused it. Leave the interpretation to the conversation with your doctor.

Turning the diary into something your doctor can read

Before an appointment, summarise rather than hand over every page. For the last month or three, note your average hot flushes per day, how many nights were disturbed, how many days you rated moderate or worse, the dates your periods started, and two or three notes that show the impact. Our GP appointment guide has a one-page summary layout and questions to bring.

Paper, spreadsheet or app?

Any of them works if you actually keep it up. Paper is quick and private but tedious to add up. A spreadsheet adds up easily but is awkward on a phone at 11pm. An app is easiest to keep going, but check where your data is stored before you choose one — health information is sensitive, and some apps send it to a server or share it with third parties.
