How to prepare for a GP appointment about perimenopause

Updated October 2026 · For the UK, Australia and Canada

General information, not medical advice. This guide is about keeping a record and preparing for an appointment. It cannot tell you whether you are in perimenopause, and it does not recommend any treatment. Speak to your GP, nurse or doctor about your own symptoms, and get help promptly for anything that worries you.

A GP appointment is short, and perimenopause symptoms rarely behave on the day you are seen. Hot flushes that woke you four times last week, a period that arrived twice in one month, a fortnight of feeling foggy — by the time you sit down, it is easy to remember only the worst bits, or to say "it's probably nothing". Arriving with a short written record changes that conversation. This guide walks through what to record, how to summarise it and what to ask.

Why your own record matters so much

There is usually no single test that settles the question. The NHS explains that there is no routine test for perimenopause and menopause, and that a doctor can usually go by your age and your symptoms (NHS: what are menopause and perimenopause).

In the UK, the NICE guideline NG23 (last updated April 2026) says that for otherwise healthy people aged 45 or over, perimenopause can be identified without laboratory tests when vasomotor symptoms (hot flushes and night sweats) have recently started alongside changes in the menstrual cycle (NICE NG23, recommendation 1.3). NICE's quality standard explains why: hormone levels such as FSH go up and down during perimenopause, so a single blood test in this age group does not help (NICE QS143, statement 1). For people aged 40 to 45, and under 40, the guideline describes a different approach that can involve blood tests — your GP will explain what applies to you.

In other words, what you describe is the evidence. A clear account of which symptoms, how often, how bad and how your periods have changed gives your doctor far more to work with than a vague sense that things are "off".

A record also helps rule other things in or out. Jean Hailes for Women's Health in Australia points out that symptoms at midlife are not always caused by menopause (Jean Hailes: menopause), and NICE notes that tests such as a blood count or thyroid function may still be needed when another cause is possible.

Step 1: track for a few weeks, ideally longer

Both Jean Hailes and the Menopause Foundation of Canada suggest keeping track for at least three months where you can (Menopause Foundation of Canada: Take Charge of Your Care). If you already have an appointment sooner, a couple of weeks is still far better than nothing. Each day, note:

  • Periods: the start date, how heavy, any spotting between periods.
  • Hot flushes and night sweats: roughly how many, and how bad the worst one was.
  • Sleep: how well you slept, and whether sweats woke you.
  • Mood, anxiety and concentration: a simple rating is enough.
  • Anything else: joint aches, headaches, palpitations, bladder or vaginal symptoms — the NHS lists many symptoms beyond hot flushes (NHS: symptoms).
  • Impact: a day off work, a cancelled plan, a night on the sofa.

Use the same scale every day (for example 0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe), and write something down on good days too. "Nothing today" is useful information. Our symptom diary guide has a full daily template.

Step 2: turn the record into one page

Nobody can read three months of daily notes in a ten-minute slot. The night before, write a summary that fits on one side:

  • Your three most bothersome symptoms, with how often they happen (for example "hot flushes: about 6 a day, most days; night sweats: woke 4 nights a week").
  • When things started, and whether they are getting better, worse or neither.
  • How your periods have changed — closer together, further apart, heavier, lighter.
  • What it is affecting: sleep, work, relationships, exercise.
  • What you have already tried, and any medicines or supplements you take, including contraception.
  • Relevant family history. The Menopause Foundation of Canada suggests noting your mother's or sisters' experience of menopause and any personal or family history of breast cancer.

Step 3: write down your questions

NICE asks clinicians to discuss the benefits and risks of each possible option with you (NICE NG23, section 1.4), so it is reasonable to come with questions. For example:

  • Could these symptoms be related to perimenopause, and is there anything else we should check?
  • What are my options, and what are the benefits and risks of each for someone like me?
  • Is there anything in my history that changes those options?
  • What should I keep tracking, and when should I come back?

Step 4: book the right appointment

When you book, it is worth asking whether a longer appointment is available, and whether anyone at the practice has a particular interest in menopause — practices differ, so simply ask. You can also bring someone with you who has seen how the symptoms affect you.

In the appointment, start with the symptom that bothers you most and its effect on your life, then hand over your one-page summary. Before you leave, make sure you know what happens next and when you will be reviewed.

Don't wait for a routine appointment if…

The NHS advises seeing a GP if you have palpitations, if you still have periods but are bleeding more than before, or if you have any vaginal bleeding after 12 months or more without a period (NHS: symptoms). Don't hold those back to collect a longer diary first.

Useful official resources by country

  • UK: NHS: menopause and perimenopause, and the NICE NG23 guideline your GP works from.
  • Australia: the Jean Hailes menopause pages and their printable symptom checklist, and the Australasian Menopause Society.
  • Canada: the Menopause Foundation of Canada care guide and Menopause and U from the Society of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists of Canada.
